# Integrals involving logarithms

## The logarithm as an antiderivative



The integral of one over x is the natural logarithm of the absolute value.

A linear inner function contributes a reciprocal factor.

The absolute value keeps the answer valid on both sides of zero.

The natural logarithm appears whenever the integrand contains a reciprocal linear factor. The absolute value is essential because the logarithm is defined on both sides of the singularity.

## Integration by parts with logarithms



Take the logarithm as the differentiated factor.

The remaining factor is often a power.

The new integral is usually polynomial.

Choosing the logarithm as the part to differentiate removes it from the remaining integral. The polynomial factor is then easy to integrate.

## Logarithmic substitution



A logarithm in the integrand suggests substituting for the logarithm.

The derivative of the logarithm supplies the remaining factor.

Convert the answer back to the original variable.

Substituting for the logarithm converts a product into a single power. The same idea handles powers of the logarithm and logarithms inside roots.
