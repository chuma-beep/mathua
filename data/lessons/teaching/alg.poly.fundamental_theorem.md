# The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

## The Statement

The fundamental theorem of algebra says that every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. Applying the theorem repeatedly shows that a polynomial of degree n has exactly n roots when they are counted with multiplicity, and it factors completely into n linear factors over the complex numbers. The result is existence, not a method: it guarantees a root without saying how to find it.

## Real and Complex Roots

Real polynomials have coefficients that are real numbers, and their nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs. If a + bi is a root, then a - bi is also a root. This pairing means the nonreal roots contribute an even count, so a real polynomial of odd degree must have at least one real root. It also means a cubic with one real root has a conjugate pair of complex roots, and its real factorisation is linear times an irreducible quadratic.

## Consequences

Multiplicity is the number of times a linear factor repeats. A root of multiplicity two counts twice in the degree count and produces a double factor. The theorem therefore explains why a degree-three polynomial cannot have four roots and why a degree-two polynomial with no real roots still has two complex roots. Finding the roots is a separate problem that uses factoring, the quadratic formula, the rational root theorem or numerical methods, but the count is settled in advance.
