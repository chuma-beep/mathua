# Classification of surfaces

## Orientable Surfaces

A closed orientable surface is determined up to homeomorphism by its genus. The sphere has genus zero, the torus has genus one, and the connected sum of g tori has genus g. The Euler characteristic is 2 minus 2g.

The genus counts handles. Adding a handle lowers the Euler characteristic by two. The sphere has characteristic two, the torus zero, and the genus-two surface minus two. This arithmetic is the quickest way to identify a surface from a triangulation.

Orientability is automatic for these surfaces. Every closed orientable surface embeds in three-dimensional space, and the inside and outside can be distinguished globally. That is why the genus alone is a complete invariant.

## Nonorientable Surfaces

A closed nonorientable surface is determined by its nonorientable genus, the number of crosscaps. The projective plane has one crosscap, the Klein bottle has two, and the connected sum of k projective planes has k. The Euler characteristic is 2 minus k.

The Möbius band is the basic nonorientable surface with boundary. Gluing a disk to its boundary gives the projective plane. Gluing two Möbius bands along their boundaries gives the Klein bottle.

Nonorientable surfaces cannot be embedded in three-dimensional space without self-intersection. They can be immersed, and the standard pictures of the projective plane and the Klein bottle show the necessary crossings.

## The Classification Theorem

Every closed connected surface is homeomorphic either to a sphere with g handles or to a sphere with k crosscaps. The two families are disjoint because orientability is a homeomorphism invariant. This is the classification theorem for surfaces.

The theorem is proved by cutting the surface into polygons and simplifying the polygon word. The normal form of the word contains either commutators, which give handles, or squares, which give crosscaps.

The theorem is a model for classification results in higher dimensions. It is also a warning: in dimension four and above no such simple classification exists, because finitely presented groups can be realised as fundamental groups of closed four-manifolds.
