# Quadric Surfaces

## Ellipsoids and Spheres

An ellipsoid is the three-dimensional analogue of an ellipse. In standard position its equation is \(x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2=1\), and all three coordinates appear squared with positive coefficients. When the three denominators are equal the surface is a sphere, so a sphere is a special ellipsoid rather than a separate family. The intercepts with the coordinate axes are at plus or minus \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), which makes the standard form easy to sketch.

A level curve at a fixed value of \(z\) is an ellipse, and the same is true at a fixed value of \(x\) or \(y\). This is the quickest way to recognise an ellipsoid: every slice by a coordinate plane is a bounded ellipse. If one denominator differs, the cross sections are still ellipses but their aspect ratio changes with height.

## Hyperboloids and Cones

A hyperboloid of one sheet has one negative term, for example \(x^2+y^2-z^2=1\). Its horizontal slices are circles that grow without bound, and the surface is connected, narrowing to a waist at \(z=0\). A hyperboloid of two sheets has two negative terms, and its slices split into two separate pieces when the remaining coordinate is small. The sign pattern in the equation is enough to tell the two cases apart.

A cone such as \(x^2+y^2=z^2\) sits between the hyperboloid families. Every slice by a plane \(z=k\) is a circle whose radius grows linearly with the height, and the vertex is the single point where all three coordinates vanish. The cone is often written as the limiting case of a hyperboloid as the constant on the right-hand side goes to zero.

## Paraboloids and Saddles

An elliptic paraboloid has the form \(z=x^2+y^2\), with only one variable raised to the first power. Its horizontal slices are circles or ellipses, and the surface opens along a single axis. A hyperbolic paraboloid has the form \(z=x^2-y^2\), whose horizontal slices are hyperbolas and whose vertical slices alternate between upward and downward parabolas. The hyperbolic paraboloid is the saddle surface that appears in multivariable optimisation.

To classify a quadric surface, first count the negative signs and the squared terms, then check whether every coordinate-plane slice is bounded. Ellipsoids are bounded in all directions, paraboloids are bounded in two directions, and hyperboloids and cones are unbounded. A quick table of sign patterns and slice types is more reliable than memorising every equation separately.
