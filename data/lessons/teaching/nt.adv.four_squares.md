# Lagrange's Four-Square Theorem

## Lagrange's Theorem

Lagrange's four-square theorem states that every nonnegative integer is a sum of four integer squares. One square is sometimes enough, two squares cover many numbers, and three squares cover all numbers except those of the form four to the a times eight b plus seven. The theorem guarantees that the fourth square is always available.

## Two and Three Squares

The proof can be organised through Euler's four-square identity, which expresses a product of two sums of four squares as another sum of four squares. It is therefore enough to prove the result for primes. A counting argument then shows that every prime is a sum of four squares, and multiplicativity extends the result to all integers.

## Counting Representations

Counting representations is a separate and more delicate question. The number of ordered representations of n as a sum of four squares is eight times the sum of the divisors of n that are not divisible by four. The formula lets you answer representation questions without listing every quadruple, and it shows how quickly the count grows with n.
