# Properties of definite integrals

This lesson develops properties of definite integrals through three connected ideas. Work through the sections in order and check each example before moving on.

## Linearity of the integral

The definite integral is linear in the integrand and in constant multipliers. If the integral of f is known, then the integral of c f is c times that value. If two integrals over the same interval are known, their sum is the integral of the sum.

Write the rule as an equation and name each symbol. The limits of integration must stay the same when the rule is applied. Changing an interval changes the value and is a separate rule.

A common error is to apply linearity to a product. The integral of a product is not the product of the integrals. Linearity covers sums and constant multiples only.

## Splitting an interval

The interval additivity rule splits a definite integral at an interior point. The integral from a to c equals the integral from a to b plus the integral from b to c, provided all three integrals exist.

The rule works in either direction because reversing the limits changes the sign. A missing piece can therefore be recovered by subtraction when the whole and one part are known.

Draw the interval and mark the split point before substituting numbers. Keeping the pieces in order prevents a sign error, which is the most common mistake in this topic.

## Symmetry shortcuts

An even function satisfies f of negative x equals f of x. Its graph is a mirror image across the vertical axis. Integrating an even function over a symmetric interval doubles the integral over the right half.

An odd function satisfies f of negative x equals negative f of x. Its graph has half-turn symmetry about the origin. Integrating an odd function over a symmetric interval gives zero whenever the integral exists.

Identify the symmetry before computing an antiderivative. A shortcut saves work and also confirms that a long calculation has the correct sign.
