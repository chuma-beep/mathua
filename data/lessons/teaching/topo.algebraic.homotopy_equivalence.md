# Homotopy equivalence

## Homotopy and Deformation

A homotopy between two maps is a continuous family of maps parametrised by an interval. Two spaces are homotopy equivalent when there are maps in both directions whose composites are homotopic to the identities. This is weaker than homeomorphism and captures the idea of deforming one space into another.

The interval is the basic example. The unit interval is homotopy equivalent to a point, because the inclusion of the point and the constant map are inverse up to homotopy. The same is true for every convex subset of Euclidean space.

Homotopy equivalence is an equivalence relation on spaces. It is compatible with composition, products and many constructions. The resulting category is coarser than the category of topological spaces and is the natural setting for algebraic topology.

## The Homotopy Category

Working up to homotopy turns topological problems into algebraic ones. Maps that are homotopic induce the same map on homology and on the fundamental group, so those invariants factor through the homotopy category.

The fundamental group is the first such invariant. A homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, and the converse is false in general: two simply connected spaces need not be homotopy equivalent. Higher homology groups are needed to distinguish them.

The homotopy category also contains the classifying spaces of groups and the Eilenberg-MacLane spaces. Those objects turn algebraic data into topological data, which is the central idea of algebraic topology.

## Invariants That Survive

Homotopy equivalence preserves the fundamental group, homology, cohomology and the Euler characteristic. It preserves connectedness and path connectedness, because both are homotopy invariant properties.

It does not preserve compactness, dimension, metric completeness or cardinality. The real line is homotopy equivalent to a point but is not compact, and the interval is homotopy equivalent to a point but has different cardinality.

When two spaces have different values of a homotopy invariant, they cannot be homotopy equivalent. That is the usual way the invariant is used. When the invariants agree, more work is needed, and often a direct construction of the equivalence is the only proof.
