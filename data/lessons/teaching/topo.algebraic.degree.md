# Degree of a map

## Winding and Degree

The degree of a map from the circle to itself counts how many times the target is wrapped, with sign. The map sending z to z to the k is the model: its degree is k. The constant map has degree zero.

For maps between spheres of the same dimension, degree is defined by the induced map on top homology. It is an integer, and it is a homotopy invariant. Two maps of the circle are homotopic exactly when they have the same degree.

Degree can also be computed by counting preimages of a regular value with signs. That formula is the bridge between the homological definition and the differential-topology definition used in degree theory.

## Properties of Degree

Degree is multiplicative under composition. The degree of a composite is the product of the degrees. It is additive under connected sums in the appropriate sense, and it changes sign when the orientation of the target is reversed.

The antipodal map on the n-sphere has degree minus one to the power n plus one. On the circle it has degree one, on the two-sphere it has degree minus one, and the sign alternates with dimension.

Degree detects surjectivity for maps of spheres. A map of nonzero degree is surjective, because a missing point would allow the map to factor through a contractible space and have degree zero.

## Computing Examples

For the map z to z to the k on the circle, degree is k. For the map z to the conjugate of z to the k, degree is minus k. For a rotation, degree is one, and for a reflection, degree is minus one.

Products of maps have degree equal to the product of the degrees. The suspension of a map of the circle has the same degree on the suspended sphere. These operations let one build examples in every dimension.

Degree is stable under homotopy, so a numerical computation can be done on any convenient representative. This is why degree is useful in fixed point theory and in the study of vector fields on manifolds.
