# Density of fractions on the number line

The fractions are dense on the number line: between any two different fractions lies another fraction. Density is the reason no smallest positive fraction exists.

## The definition

Take two fractions \(\frac{a}{b}\) and \(\frac{c}{d}\) with \(\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}\) and positive denominators. Their average, written as \(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d})\), is again a fraction. It is larger than the left endpoint because the endpoints differ and it is smaller than the right endpoint for the same reason. Repeating the construction gives a new fraction each time, so any gap holds infinitely many fractions.

## An example

Between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\) the midpoint is \(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{3}{8}\). The three values in order are \(\frac{1}{4}\), \(\frac{3}{8}\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\). Averaging again puts \(\frac{5}{16}\) between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{3}{8}\). No fraction with denominator 4 sits strictly between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\), yet \(\frac{3}{8}\) does, so density belongs to the fractions and not to one chosen denominator.

## Why it matters

Density explains why a ruler marked every \(\frac{1}{8}\) inch still leaves room for lengths in between. It also explains why the next fraction after \(\frac{1}{2}\) has no answer, unlike the whole numbers, where 3 is followed by 4. Rounding to a fixed grid therefore always discards information.

Because no fraction is smallest, every measurement is an approximation to a chosen precision.
