# Mass from a radial density

## Density on a disk

When density depends only on distance from the centre, a thin ring of radius \(r\) and width \(dr\) carries mass \(\rho(r) \cdot 2\pi r\,dr\). The ring's area is what makes the extra factor \(r\) appear. A disk of radius 2 cut into rings of width 0.1 holds about 20 rings.

## The radial integral

The mass is the radial integral \(M = 2\pi\int_0^R r\rho(r)\,dr\). For \(\rho(r) = 1 + r\) on a disk of radius 2 the integral is \(2\pi\int_0^2 (r + r^2)dr = 2\pi(2 + 8/3) = 28\pi/3 \approx 29.32\). The density grows outward here, so the mass exceeds the constant-density value \(4\pi \approx 12.57\) at density 1.

## A constant density

Constant density is the check case. With \(\rho = 3\) and \(R = 2\) the integral gives \(2\pi \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 12\pi \approx 37.70\), which is the density times the area \(4\pi\). The radial formula reduces to that product, as it must. Doubling the radius multiplies the mass by four, as the area does.

A complete answer names the relation, substitutes the data and states the result in the language of the problem. When a second method is available, compare the two results before moving on.
