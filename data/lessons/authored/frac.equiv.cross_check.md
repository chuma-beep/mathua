# Checking equivalence by cross multiplication

Two fractions are equal exactly when their cross products agree. The test replaces an equivalence question with one multiplication on each side.

## The rule

For fractions \(\frac{a}{b}\) and \(\frac{c}{d}\) with nonzero denominators, \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\) holds exactly when \(ad = bc\). Multiplying both sides by the product \(bd\), which is nonzero, clears both denominators and leaves that equation. The rule works for negative fractions as well, because the multiplier is nonzero and no inequality is reversed. The pair \(\frac{2}{3}\) and \(\frac{4}{6}\) passes because \(2 \times 6 = 12\) and \(3 \times 4 = 12\).

## A worked example

Test \(\frac{6}{8}\) against \(\frac{9}{12}\). The cross products are \(6 \times 12 = 72\) and \(8 \times 9 = 72\), so the fractions are equal. Both reduce to \(\frac{3}{4}\), which confirms the test. The pair \(\frac{4}{6}\) and \(\frac{6}{9}\) also passes, with 36 on each side. A pair such as \(\frac{3}{5}\) and \(\frac{5}{8}\) gives 24 against 25 and is not equal.

## Checking the result

Reduce each fraction to lowest terms as a second check or divide both products by a common factor. When the products differ, their order also tells which fraction is larger, so the test answers a comparison at the same time. For the pair \(\frac{3}{5}\) and \(\frac{5}{8}\), the products 24 and 25 differ, so the values differ. A zero denominator invalidates the test, so check the denominators first.

Cross multiplication is reliable because it is only a cleared-denominator comparison.
