# Repeating decimals as fractions

## Why 0.333... Equals One Third

A repeating decimal hides an infinite sum. Algebra can collapse it. Let \\(x = 0.333...\\). Multiply by 10, which shifts the digits one place: \\(10x = 3.333...\\). Subtract the first equation from the second and the tails cancel, leaving \\(9x = 3\\).

Divide both sides by 9 to get \\(x = \\frac{3}{9}\\), which reduces to \\(\\frac{1}{3}\\). The step works because the repeating block has one digit, so one shift aligns identical tails. A two-digit block needs a shift of two places.

## Repeating Nines and Repeated Digits

The same method settles the strange case of 0.999... Let \\(x = 0.999...\\), so \\(10x = 9.999...\\). Subtracting gives \\(9x = 9\\), so \\(x = 1\\). The decimal and the whole number name one value, which is why two different decimal strings can represent the same point.

A repeated block of two digits needs a shift of two places. For 0.272727..., write \\(x = 0.272727...\\) and \\(100x = 27.272727...\\). Then \\(99x = 27\\), so \\(x = \\frac{27}{99} = \\frac{3}{11}\\).

## Writing a Repeating Decimal as a Fraction

For 0.1666..., only the 6 repeats, so shift past the non-repeating digit first. Let \\(x = 0.1666...\\), then \\(10x = 1.666...\\) and \\(100x = 16.666...\\). Subtracting the two shifted forms gives \\(90x = 15\\), so \\(x = \\frac{15}{90} = \\frac{1}{6}\\).

Check by dividing: 1 divided by 6 gives 0.1666... . The denominator always comes from the difference of the two powers of ten, which is 90 here.
