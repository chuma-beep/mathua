# The Stolz-Cesaro theorem

The Stolz-Cesaro theorem is the discrete analogue of L'Hopital's rule. It replaces the limit of a ratio by the limit of the ratio of consecutive differences.

## The ratio

For \(a_n=n^{p}\) and \(b_n=n^{p+1}\), the difference ratio tends to \(p/(p+1)\). The theorem says the original ratio has the same limit.

## The exponent

If \(b_n=n^{k}\) grows and \(a_n=n^{k+1}\), the difference ratio tends to \(k+1\). The exponent of the numerator is one above the exponent of the denominator.

## The geometric case

For \(a_n=q^{n}\) and \(b_n=p^{n}\) with \(q<p\), the difference ratio tends to \(q/p\). The limit is the ratio of the two bases, not of the two values.

To check your understanding, form consecutive differences, compare the powers and divide the bases.

A ratio of sequences has the same limit as the ratio of their differences.

Each section above supplies the reasoning for one practice family, so work a numeric instance of each before moving on. Write the first step of every instance down before you compute the final value, because the grader checks the number but the method is what the next concept assumes. If an instance refuses to settle, redraw it with smaller numbers and check the sign of every term; the arithmetic in these families is exact, so a mismatch means a sign or an order of operations, not an approximation.
