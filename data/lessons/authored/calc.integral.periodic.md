# Integrals of periodic functions

Every period of a periodic function contributes the same integral. That fact turns an integral over many periods into one period multiplied by the count.

## Many periods

If the integral over one period is \(I\), the integral over \(k\) periods is \(kI\). The period count multiplies and never changes the shape.

## Splitting a period

If the first half of a period contributes \(J\) and the whole period contributes \(I\), the second half contributes \(I-J\). The two halves add to the whole.

## The average

The average value over one period is the integral divided by the period. For a period \(T\) and integral \(I\), the average is \(I/T\).

To check your understanding, integrate one period, subtract the first half and divide by the period.

Periodicity turns a long integral into a multiplication.

Each section above supplies the reasoning for one practice family, so work a numeric instance of each before moving on. Write the first step of every instance down before you compute the final value, because the grader checks the number but the method is what the next concept assumes. If an instance refuses to settle, redraw it with smaller numbers and check the sign of every term; the arithmetic in these families is exact, so a mismatch means a sign or an order of operations, not an approximation.
