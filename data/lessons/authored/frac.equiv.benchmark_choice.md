# Choosing a benchmark to compare fractions

A benchmark is a familiar value such as 0, \(\frac{1}{2}\) or 1 that a fraction can be measured against. Placing two fractions relative to the same benchmark often settles a comparison without a common denominator.

## The rule

Compare each fraction with the chosen benchmark first. If one lies above the benchmark and the other below, the order is immediate. If both lie on the same side, compare their distances from the benchmark: for two fractions above \(\frac{1}{2}\), the one farther above is larger.

## A worked example

Compare \(\frac{5}{9}\) with \(\frac{6}{11}\) using \(\frac{1}{2}\). The first exceeds one half because \(5 \times 2 = 10\) is greater than 9 and the second exceeds it because \(6 \times 2 = 12\) is greater than 11. The distances are \(\frac{5}{9} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{18}\) and \(\frac{6}{11} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{22}\). Since \(\frac{1}{18}\) is larger than \(\frac{1}{22}\), the fraction \(\frac{5}{9}\) sits farther above the benchmark and is the larger value.

## Checking the result

Subtract the two fractions over the common denominator 99: \(\frac{55}{99} - \frac{54}{99} = \frac{1}{99}\), a positive difference, so \(\frac{5}{9}\) is larger. Cross multiplication agrees, because \(5 \times 11 = 55\) exceeds \(9 \times 6 = 54\) by exactly 1.

A benchmark turns a comparison into two short distances from a value you already know.
