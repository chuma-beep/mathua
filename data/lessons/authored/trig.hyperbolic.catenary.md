# The catenary curve

A catenary is the curve a uniform chain takes when it hangs under its own weight, written \(y = a\cosh(x/a)\) with the lowest point at \(x = 0\).

## The hanging chain equation

The constant \(a\) is the tension at the lowest point divided by the weight per unit length, so it
sets the scale of the whole curve. For \(a = 2\) the equation is \(y = 2\cosh(x/2)\). At the
lowest point \(x = 0\) the height is two; at \(x = 2\) it is \(2\cosh 1 \approx 3.086\). A large
scale gives a flat chain, a small one falls away sharply.

## Height along the curve

The height grows symmetrically and almost quadratically near the bottom. At \(x = 4\) the height
is 7.524, which is 4.438 more than the height at \(x = 2\). The quadratic approximation
\(a + x^{2}/(2a)\) gives 3 at \(x = 2\), against the exact 3.086. That is close enough to explain
why a hanging chain can look like a parabola over a short span but far enough to matter over a
long one.

## Arc length of a catenary

The arc length from the lowest point is \(s = a\sinh(x/a)\), which grows faster than the
horizontal distance. For \(a = 2\) and \(x = 2\) the length is 2.350. Over the stretch from
\(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\) the length is \(2\sinh 2 \approx 7.254\), against a straight distance of
only four.

Height, slope and arc length all come from the same single scale \(a\).
