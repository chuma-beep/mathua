# Intervals of continuity

Most familiar functions are continuous wherever they are defined. Finding the intervals of continuity therefore starts with finding the domain, then checking whether the domain breaks into separate pieces.

## Denominators exclude points

A rational function is continuous wherever its denominator is nonzero. Set the denominator equal to zero and solve. Each solution is a point removed from the domain, and the function is continuous on the intervals between them.

For \(1/(x-3)\), the only gap is \(x=3\). The function is continuous on \((-\infty,3)\) and on \((3,\infty)\), but not across the gap. The point itself is not in the domain, so continuity there is not a question.

## Domains with endpoints

A square root requires a nonnegative radicand. The solution of the inequality is a closed interval, and the function is continuous on that interval from its left endpoint onward. The endpoint belongs to the domain even though the derivative may fail there.

A logarithm requires a positive argument, which gives an open interval. The endpoint is excluded, so the interval of continuity is open at that end. Reading the domain carefully prevents adding or dropping an endpoint.

## Several gaps at once

A denominator with several factors removes several points. The domain splits into more intervals, and the function is continuous on each one separately. A repeated factor removes the point once; it does not create a second gap.

A function built from several pieces can also have a gap at a boundary where the pieces disagree. In that case the domain may be connected while the graph is not. Continuity is about the values, not only about where the formula is defined.
