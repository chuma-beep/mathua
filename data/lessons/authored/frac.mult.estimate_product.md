# Estimating a product of fractions

An estimate for a product rounds each factor to a benchmark before multiplying. Because both factors lie between 0 and 1 in many cases, the product is smaller than either one.

## The method

Round each factor to 0, \(\frac{1}{2}\) or 1, then multiply the rounded values. A factor rounded upward pushes the estimate up and a factor rounded downward pushes it down. When both move the same way the error grows and when they move in opposite directions it shrinks.

## A worked product

Estimate \(\frac{11}{12} \times \frac{5}{8}\). The first factor rounds to 1 and the second to \(\frac{1}{2}\), so the estimate is \(\frac{1}{2}\). The exact product is \(\frac{55}{96}\), about 0.573, so the estimate is low by about 0.073. A second case is \(\frac{7}{8} \times \frac{5}{6}\), where both factors round to 1 and the estimate is 1, while the exact product is \(\frac{35}{48}\), about 0.729.

## Checking the result

Bracket the first product. A lower bound is \(\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{8}\) and an upper bound is \(1 \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{4}\). The exact value \(\frac{55}{96}\) lies between \(\frac{36}{96}\) and \(\frac{72}{96}\), so the estimate sits inside the bracket.

An estimate is a size and a bracket is a guarantee.
