# Equivalent fractions in a chain

Equivalent fractions form a chain in which each term comes from the one before by multiplying both parts by the same nonzero number. Every term in the chain names the same value.

## The rule

If \(\frac{a}{b}\) is a fraction and \(k\) is a nonzero whole number, then \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{ka}{kb}\). Repeating with different multipliers builds a chain such as \(\frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{8}{12}\). Dividing both parts by a common factor walks the chain in the other direction, so the relation is reversible.

## A worked example

Start from \(\frac{2}{3}\) and extend it to denominator 15. The multiplier is 5 because \(3 \times 5 = 15\), so the numerator becomes \(2 \times 5 = 10\) and the term is \(\frac{10}{15}\). Extending again by 2 gives \(\frac{20}{30}\). The chain is \(\frac{2}{3} = \frac{10}{15} = \frac{20}{30}\) and the middle term also connects to \(\frac{4}{6}\) by a multiplier of 2 on the first term.

## Checking the result

Cross multiply adjacent terms: \(2 \times 15 = 30\) and \(3 \times 10 = 30\), then \(10 \times 30 = 300\) and \(15 \times 20 = 300\). Reducing each term by its multiplier returns \(\frac{2}{3}\), so the chain closes at the start. A term that fails one cross product is not on the chain, even if its denominator looks right.

A chain is one value wearing several names and each name is a legal multiplication.
