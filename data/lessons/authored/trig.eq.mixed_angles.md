# Equations with mixed angles

A mixed-angle equation contains two different multiples of the unknown, such as \(2x\) beside \(x\); the method reduces both to a single angle before solving.

## A double angle beside a single angle

The double-angle identity is what removes the mismatch. For \(\sin 2x = \cos x\), expand the left
side as \(2\sin x\cos x\) and collect: \(\cos x(2\sin x - 1) = 0\). So \(\cos x = 0\) at 90 and
270 degrees. Also \(\sin x = 1/2\) at 30 and 150 degrees. Direct substitution confirms each one:
at 30 degrees both sides equal 0.8660.

## Reducing to one angle

A double angle in the other direction is just as useful. The equation \(2\cos^{2}x + \cos 2x = 2\)
becomes \(4\cos^{2}x - 1 = 2\) once the identity \(\cos 2x = 2\cos^{2}x - 1\) is applied. Then
\(\cos x = \pm\sqrt{3}/2\). The solutions are 30, 150, 210 and 330 degrees. Every term now
contains the same angle. The rest is quadratic work that the equation already taught.

## Counting solutions in a turn

A multiple angle covers several turns while the unknown covers one. For \(\sin 3x = 1/2\), the
angles \(3x\) are 30, 150, 390, 510, 750 and 870 degrees. Dividing by 3 gives 10, 50, 130, 170,
250 and 290 degrees, which makes six solutions in all. The count is six because \(3x\) sweeps
three full turns inside one turn of \(x\). That is the pattern for every multiple angle.

Count the solutions of the multiple angle first, then divide.
