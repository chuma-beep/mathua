# Proper and improper fractions

A proper fraction has a numerator smaller than its denominator, so its value is less than 1. An improper fraction has a numerator at least as large as its denominator, so its value is 1 or more.

## The definition

For positive whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\), the fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) is proper when \(a < b\) and improper when \(a \ge 1\). The weak inequality matters: \(\frac{4}{4}\) equals 1 and counts as improper. A proper fraction covers part of one whole, while an improper fraction covers one whole or several wholes. The denominator stays positive, so the classification never depends on a sign.

## An example

Of the fractions \(\frac{2}{3}\), \(\frac{9}{8}\), \(\frac{5}{5}\) and \(\frac{7}{4}\), only \(\frac{2}{3}\) is proper. The others rewrite as \(1\frac{1}{8}\), 1 and \(1\frac{3}{4}\). In order of value they are \(\frac{2}{3}\), \(\frac{5}{5}\), \(\frac{9}{8}\) and \(\frac{7}{4}\). The decimal forms 0.667, 1, 1.125 and 1.75 confirm the order.

## Why it matters

The classification is a fast size check before any calculation. It also decides which form to use: a mixed number reads naturally in a measurement, while an improper fraction is easier to multiply or divide because it is a single numerator over a single denominator.

Knowing which side of 1 a fraction sits on prevents most estimation errors.
