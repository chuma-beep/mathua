# Limits by conjugates

A conjugate removes a square-root difference that would otherwise give zero over zero.

## Multiplying by the conjugate

For a difference \(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}\), multiply numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}\). The product is \(A-B\), which often factors.

The conjugate is the same two terms with the sign between them changed. It rationalizes the numerator.

Multiplying by the conjugate is valid because the conjugate is nonzero near the limit point.

## Cancelling the zero factor

After rationalizing, the factor that caused the zero in the denominator usually cancels. The remaining expression can be evaluated by substitution.

The cancelled factor is not zero at the limit point in the simplified expression. The original function was undefined there.

The limit can exist even when the function value does not, which is the idea of a removable discontinuity.

## The one-sided form

With \(\sqrt{x}\), the domain is \(x\ge0\). At a boundary point only one side is available, so the limit is one-sided.

For \((x-a^2)/(\sqrt{x}-a)\), the conjugate gives a limit of \(2a\). The direction matters only if the domain is restricted.

Check the domain before deciding whether a two-sided limit is meaningful.
