# ADR-057: Calculus is three courses, so it is three domains

*2026-10-30*

**The `calculus` domain becomes `calc1`, `calc2` and `calc3`, and the concept ids do not change.** The corpus already had three courses, `calc1` "Calculus I", `calc2` "Calculus II" and `calc3` "Calculus III", but one domain held all 205 entries. That made the curriculum picker show a single row where a learner thinks in semesters, and it made the diagnostic scope, the weakness report and the graph shade unable to distinguish a first course from a third. The domain field is the classification the product reads, so it now names the stage. The id prefix stays `calc.` because an id is durable history: attempts, progress rows, lesson mappings and generator registrations are keyed by it, and renaming 205 ids would buy nothing that changing one field does not.

**The split follows the courses, not the subdomains.** The corpus is organised into nine topic buckets, and a topic is not a stage: derivatives appear in all three courses, integrals appear in Calculus I and Calculus II, and sequences appear in Calculus II and Calculus III. The boundary that a learner experiences is the course, so a concept belongs to the earliest course whose path needs it. A direct course target wins over everything. A concept that is not a target but sits in a course's prerequisite closure belongs to that course, earliest first. This is why `calc.integral.indefinite` is in Calculus I even though its subdomain is `calculus.integrals`: the definite integral and the Fundamental Theorem are Calculus I targets and they depend on it. The result is 76 concepts in `calc1`, 63 in `calc2` and 61 in `calc3`.

**Five concepts left the calculus domain entirely.** `calc.limit.supremum`, `calc.limit.uniform_continuity`, `calc.integral.riemann_criteria`, `calc.seq.cauchy` and `calc.series.cauchy_criterion` are reached only through the Real Analysis course, which uses them as the bridge from its own `ra.*` concepts. They keep their `calc.` ids and move to the `real_analysis` domain, which grows from 50 to 55. Keeping them in a calculus domain would have made a learner browsing Calculus III meet the Cauchy criterion, and moving them into `calc3` would have misrepresented what that course assesses. The id prefix and the domain are different facts: one records where a concept came from, the other where it is taught now.

**The course targets were corrected first, because a split publishes whatever boundaries it is given.** Five multivariable targets sat in `calc1`: `calc.deriv.gradient`, `calc.deriv.directional`, `calc.deriv.chain_multi`, `calc.deriv.clairaut` and `calc.deriv.total_diff`. MIT 18.02 requires 18.01 as a prerequisite and teaches exactly this material, so they moved to `calc3`. Three ordinary Calculus I topics were reachable only through Real Analysis and are now `calc1` targets: L'Hopital's rule, indeterminate forms and the epsilon-delta definition of a limit. Four ordinary Calculus II topics moved into `calc2`: geometric series, the ratio test, bounded sequences and monotone sequences. `calc.deriv.implicit` was already in the Calculus I closure through implicit differentiation of the second derivative, and it is now a target as well.

**The placement of the twelve is what the standards say, with one judgement call.** MIT 18.01 puts L'Hopital in its fifth unit and OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 puts it at 4.8; the College Board places it in AP Calculus AB Unit 4. OpenStax Calculus Volume 2 treats geometric series at 5.2 and the ratio test at 5.6, and AP Calculus BC places both in Unit 10. OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, section 5.1 states the Monotone Convergence Theorem and defines bounded sequences, so those two are Calculus II even though their proofs belong to analysis. Supremum and infimum, uniform continuity, Cauchy sequences, the Cauchy criterion for series and the Riemann integrability criterion are MIT 18.100A material, in weeks 2, 5, 9 and 11, and in Lebl's *Basic Analysis* chapters 1, 2, 3 and 5. Epsilon-delta is the contested one: OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 has it as section 2.5, while MIT 18.01 and AP Calculus do not. The repo had already decided the question in practice, because `calc.deriv.epsilon_delta_deriv` was a `calc1` target while the limit definition it depends on was not. Adding the limit version makes the pair coherent.

**The picker groups the three domains under one heading.** Three consecutive top-level rows would read as three unrelated subjects, so `/domains` renders a "Calculus" heading before `calc1`, `calc2` and `calc3`. The grouping is derived from the id prefix and adds no schema field. The rows underneath keep their own counts, and opening one still scopes the topic list to that course. This is the part of the change that answers the navigation objection to splitting: the subject list stays calm while the reports and the graph get the granularity.

**Nothing about a learner's history moves.** Progress is keyed by concept id, so the migration is a metadata edit rather than a database one. A learner who mastered `calc.deriv.chain_rule` under the old domain still has that mastery; the concept is now listed under Calculus I, where it was always taught. The change does move the course totals, because `ProgressForCourse` counts the transitive path: `calc1` goes from 147 to 143 concepts, `calc2` from 134 to 138 and `calc3` from 148 to 153, as targets leave one course or arrive in another. The totals are not comparable to the old ones, and the docs quote the new ones rather than pretending the boundary did not move.

**What this does not do.** It adds no concepts, lessons or questions. The calculus corpus remains 200 entries in the three domains plus the five in Real Analysis. The measured weakness is question variety, not coverage: 47 of the 205 calculus concepts produce fewer than twelve distinct situations in sixty draws, and that is the next piece of work. Splitting a domain cannot make a generator less repetitive, and treating the new rows as progress on content depth would be a category error.

**Alternatives considered.** Keeping one domain and grouping its topics by course in the UI was the cheaper change, and it was rejected because the diagnostic, the weakness report and the graph all read the domain field; a presentation-only grouping would leave every one of them coarse. Fixing the courses without splitting the domain was the smallest correct change, and it is part of this decision rather than an alternative to it. Splitting by subdomain was rejected because a subdomain is a topic, and the nine topic buckets do not line up with the three stages: integrals and sequences each span two courses, and derivatives span all three.
